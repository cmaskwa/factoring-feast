📖 Grandma's Factoring Cookbook

Two family recipes for factoring. Always check the leftovers: a difference of two squares means you need to factor again!

Cubic

Recipe #1: Factor by Grouping

For cubics with four terms

Ingredients: x3 + 5x2 − 9x − 45
1

Split into two groups — the first two terms and the last two terms.

(x3 + 5x2) + (−9x − 45)
2

Pull out the GCF of each group. If the second group starts with a minus, pull out a negative GCF.

x2(x + 5) − 9(x + 5)
3

Factor out the matching binomial. Both groups share (x + 5).

(x + 5)(x2 − 9)
4

Check the leftovers! x2 − 9 is a difference of two squares: a2 − b2 = (a − b)(a + b)

(x + 5)(x − 3)(x + 3)
👩‍🍳 Chef's tip: If the two binomials in step 2 don't match, double-check the sign of the GCF you pulled out of the second group.
Quartic

Recipe #2: Quadratic Form

For quartics like ax4 + bx2 + c

Ingredients: x4 − 10x2 + 9
1

Substitute u = x2, so x4 = u2.

u2 − 10u + 9
2

Factor like a quadratic. Two numbers that multiply to 9 and add to −10: −1 and −9.

(u − 1)(u − 9)
3

Put x2 back in for u.

(x2 − 1)(x2 − 9)
4

Check the leftovers! Both factors are differences of two squares.

(x − 1)(x + 1)(x − 3)(x + 3)
👩‍🍳 Special side dish: x4 − 81 is already a difference of squares: (x2 − 9)(x2 + 9) → (x − 3)(x + 3)(x2 + 9). A sum of squares like x2 + 9 does not factor, and neither does x2 − 2 (2 isn't a perfect square) — so you stop there.

⌨️ How to type your answers

Exponentsx^2 means x2
Each factor in parentheses(x+5)(x-3)(x+3)
Order doesn't matter(x+3)(x-3)(x+5) is also correct
Repeated factorsYou may write (x+1)^2 or (x+1)(x+1)
